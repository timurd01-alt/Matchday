TRANSPARENT WEEKLY SCORECARD · THE INDEPENDENT AUDITOR

The best call and the worst miss wore the same uniform.

July 26–August 1, 2026Published August 2Completed seven-day window

The official result is 34 hits and 28 misses from 62 verified, locked, graded MLB picks: 54.8%. That percentage is a description, not a victory lap. It is one short weekly sample, it has no valid market baseline, and its most important diagnostic is uncomfortable: the highest-confidence band performed worst.

Integrity flag — confidence did not travel. The seven calls made at 80–89% confidence averaged 81.1% stated probability but won only 42.9% of the time. That is a 38.3-point overstatement in a seven-game slice. It is not proof of a permanent flaw, but it is the central failure this week.

Every counted pick carried the current-schema integrity marker, an UPCOMING status at lock, and a lock time before its scheduled first pitch (0.59 to 11.99 hours early). All 62 were graded against the same two-way final-winner settlement used for the MLB pick; no legacy rows were added and no pick was rewritten. BALLDONTLIE is documented in the site’s compliance record as the approved MLB fixture/result provider.

The Brier figure is the stable three-outcome-form calculation: squared error across home, draw, and away probabilities, with MLB’s draw probability fixed at zero; lower is better. Log loss is the negative log probability assigned to the realized winner, also lower is better. Neither metric says the model improved, and neither should be read apart from the sample size.

The evidence is too small to fit or claim a correction. It is large enough to reject any boast based on the headline hit rate. In particular, the 70%+ calls went 14–13, not like a high-confidence group. Next week will use these exact definitions again; it will not relabel confidence after the fact.

Two Boston calls, two different lessons

The best call was Boston at Oakland on July 28: an 83% Boston pick that won 4–2. The largest miss came roughly a day later in the same matchup: another 83% Boston pick, this time losing 4–3. The point is not that the model “almost” got the miss right. A loss at 83% carries a 1.772 log-loss penalty, versus 0.186 for the hit, precisely because the forecast expressed so much certainty.

Market comparison, corrections, and limits

There are two stored market-reference rows: the model and market each went 1–1. Neither carries a market snapshot timestamp, so they do not clear the same pregame-proof rule and are not a valid model-versus-market comparison. The honest comparison sample is therefore n=0, not a tie.

Seven additional verified locks in the window had no final grade at audit time and were excluded. No result corrections were recorded in the eligible cohort. The append-only MLB ledger currently contains 26 of the 62 counted fixture locks; the remaining 36 have the required lock fields and frozen snapshots in the official pick log but await ledger reconciliation. That lag limits independent reproduction of the full cohort and is an integrity issue to resolve, not a reason to silently alter this week’s score.

The Ombudsman’s note. The record is mixed, and the high-confidence portion is poor. We are publishing both because a weekly scorecard should make the failure as easy to find as the hits. This report does not claim an improvement, a market edge, or a calibrated model from one week of baseball.

The basics

What is Matchday?

A free research site for college football and men's college basketball: power ratings, win probabilities, projected scores, rankings and a pick record graded in public. It's built for fans who want to understand a game, not to sell anything.

Who builds and runs Matchday?

One person, as a passion project rather than a company. It makes no money and has no plans to: no ads, no subscriptions, no affiliate links, no paid placements. It is not affiliated with the NCAA, its conferences or schools, or any bookmaker.

How can I use it?

However works for you: settle an argument, follow a team, check how the model has really done, or compare it with the betting market. Nothing here is betting advice, and nobody with a stake in what you decide pays for the site.

The model

Where do the picks come from?

From Matchday's own rating model. Every pick, probability, projected score and rating on the site comes from it; no other published rating or index is fed in.

The win probability starts from the gap between the two teams' ratings plus home field. Where a betting market price exists, the published number blends the model (60%) with the market (40%), because the market carries public information the model can't see, such as late injury news. The model's own number is kept and graded too, and the Model-Market Watch shows where the two disagree.

What is a power rating?

How many points better than an average team a side would be on a neutral field against an average schedule. It is built from game results and adjusted for opponent strength, so a blowout of a weak team counts for less than a close win over a strong one. Each team also gets an offense and defense rating and a strength-of-schedule figure.

How does the model rate teams before they've played?

In football, the preseason rating starts from last season's and is adjusted for the roster: how much production returned, what arrived through transfers, the roster's talent and recruiting class, and whether the starting quarterback is new. As games are played, results take over and the preseason rating fades out. FCS teams are rated from their own games too, not lumped together.

In basketball, the preseason projects each player on the roster (returning players, transfers and freshmen) and adds them up.

What does a win probability mean?

It's the chance the model gives a team to win, not a guarantee. It's checked against results: over this season, teams the model put at about 75% have won about 75% of the time. A 60% pick is expected to lose four times in ten.

How is the projected score made?

The model's expected margin and total are played out 10,000 times with realistic game-to-game variation, and the middle result is shown, rounded to a score football actually produces. Totals are read against this season's real scoring level. The score follows the model's line rather than assuming the favourite wins, so a close game is shown as a close game. Basketball scores, which don't cluster the way football scores do, are shown straight from the expected margin and total.

Why does the model sometimes disagree with the market?

The market can price in things the model doesn't see yet, like injury news. The model can see things the market is slow to move on. The goal is to be right before the market is, measured honestly, so disagreements are tracked and graded on their own rather than hidden inside an overall hit rate.

Picks and the record

When are picks locked?

Sixty minutes before kickoff. The scorecard freezes the newest forecast made by then, together with the market price at that moment, and never rewrites it. Picks can still update before the lock as new information arrives; after it, nothing changes.

How is the record graded?

Against official final scores, and every locked pick counts, including the ones that look bad afterwards. Alongside the hit rate, the record compares the model's probabilities with the market's. A hit rate alone flatters any model that mostly agrees with the favourite, so the picks where the model and the market disagree are reported separately.

What are the Upsets of the week?

Up to three underdogs a week that the market prices below 35% and the model rates at least 5 points higher. A thin week posts fewer rather than padding the list. They are graded against how often the market said those underdogs would win, which is the honest baseline: most underdogs lose, so the question is whether these win more often than their prices implied.

Rankings

What is the difference between the Top 25s?

The power rating Top 25 is the model's own ranking from its ratings. The AP Top 25 is the official national media poll, shown with the date it was released. The Personal Ballot is one person's weekly Top 25, checked against each team's record and résumé before it is published. It's an opinion, and it is labelled as one.

Data and privacy

Where does the data come from?

Free and free-tier sports data providers, public facts such as final scores and the AP poll, a betting-market odds provider, and public news headlines. The full list of sources and their terms is on the Legal page.

Do I need an account, and is my data private?

No account is needed. Settings such as a favourite team stay in your own browser. Signing in is optional and only keeps your community picks across devices. Details are in the Legal page's privacy section.

How often does it update?

Scores and odds refresh about every hour. Ratings, the Top 25s and the coming week's picks are rebuilt every Sunday after the weekend's games. Matchday isn't a live-score service: a game in progress shows as pending until its final score arrives.
